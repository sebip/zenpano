You don't need a newer drone: the full quality is already on your card

zenpano helps you get it out, on your phone or your PC.

Many DJI pilots post a 360 sphere straight from DJI Fly (or DJI GO 4 on older drones like the Mavic Air and Mavic Pro), spot soft rooftops or a white patch by the sun, and assume they need a newer drone. Usually, they don't. DJI Fly works fine for a quick share after landing, but your drone already records the source frames for a sharper sphere. The difference comes from processing the files already on your SD card.

What actually ends up on Facebook

The sphere DJI Fly saves is a compressed preview. On a phone screen it looks passable. Zoom in on a house, street, or path, and details blur. Next to the sun, the sky clips to white. Over water, the far shore can step up or down at a seam. The camera isn't at fault: the app saves the sphere small from compressed photos.

What is already sitting on your SD card

When your drone shoots a sphere, it stores the individual frames in DCIM\PANORAMA, one subfolder per flight. A Mini 2, Mini 2 SE, or Mini 4K saves 26 frames (DJI_0001 to DJI_0026) as JPG and, with RAW enabled, as DNG. A Mini 3 Pro or Mini 4 Pro saves 35 frames, as DNG when the panorama format is set to RAW. A Mavic Air saves 25 frames. Copy that subfolder to your phone or PC before formatting the card.

DJI Fly outputs 4096 × 2048 px on the Mini 2, Mini 2 SE, and Mini 4K; 8192 × 4096 px on the Mini 3 Pro; and 12000 × 6000 px on the Mini 4 Pro. When I stitch the original DNGs in zenpano, a Mini 2 sphere reaches 16384 × 8192 px (four times the width), and Mini 3 Pro or Mini 4 Pro spheres reach about 16500 × 8250 px.

The usual way, and the short way

Getting a really good sphere out of those frames has always taken several programs: develop the RAW files in one, stitch them in another, then mask out what moved, fill the zenith (the hole above the drone) and edit the result in yet another. That can take hours for one sphere, and a lot to learn. zenpano does the developing, the stitching, the repairs and the zenith in one app, on the phone or the PC, in a few minutes; on the PC there is a quick edit too.

From card to finished sphere on your phone

In zenpano on Android, pick the folder, choose the source, and start—no PC needed. You choose the source by how much time you have and what the sphere is for:

  • From JPG frames: the fast route. Stitching the card's JPGs gives cleaner seams and more detail than DJI Fly—about 10752 px wide on a Mini 2, Mini 2 SE or Mini 4K instead of 4096 px (their panorama JPGs are only 2000 × 1500). On the Mavic Air the frames are full-size JPGs, so even the fast route gives the full 16524 px.
  • From DNG frames (DNG+ is the same with extra care for skies and shadows, made for difficult light): full resolution, or sized for Kuula (16384 px), Google Maps (13312 px), or an MB cap, keeping highlight and shadow detail. The «Shadow recovery» and «Highlight reduction» sliders lift dark areas and pull back bright ones during raw development, right on the phone. On my phone, the 26 DNG frames of a Mini 2 sphere take about four minutes with DNG+.

A zenpano sphere looks flatter at first than DJI Fly on purpose. DJI Fly bakes in heavy contrast, clipping highlights and crushing shadows. I keep that tonal range intact so you can edit it yourself.

Closing the sky, and hiding the propellers

Whichever source you choose, the last step is closing the sky. Drones cannot shoot straight up, leaving an overhead gap. DJI Fly blurs it and most desktop stitchers leave it black. I built zenpano to fill the zenith to match the surrounding sky for 360 viewers. On a windy flight the drone tilts and the propellers can show at the top of the frames; raising the zenith «Margin» (or dragging the orange line in the preview) leaves them out, and the filled sky covers that part too.

When something moves between shots

While the drone rotates, cars, people, waves, clouds, or nearby trees move. That leaves doubled objects, broken lines, or smeared seams. DJI Fly has no fix for it.

In zenpano, Repair an area lets you mark the spot and pick a clean version from an overlapping frame without touching the rest of the panorama. It depends on what the overlapping shots captured, so not every moving subject can be fixed, though many seam breaks take a single repair.

More than 360 spheres

zenpano also stitches the drone's Wide-angle and 180° panoramas: on the Mini 2, Mini 2 SE and Mini 4K, and in beta on the Air 3, Mini 5 Pro and Mavic Pro. On the Mavic Air, Wide-angle and Vertical.

From any finished sphere, Crop exports regular photos without altering the sphere itself. Frame a straight-line wide shot, a 180° band, or a rectangle in Full, 2:1, 16:9, 3:2, 4:3, or 1:1. Choose how the view is drawn (straight lines kept straight, or a wider curved look), and let Auto orientation level the horizon.

You can also render a little planet and rotate it to put the view you want on top (the «Roll» slider, or two fingers on the phone), at full resolution (around 16000 × 16000 px) or smaller (8192 px, 4096 px, or a file-size cap; 9 MB by default so Facebook treats it as a normal photo).

On Windows, when you want more control

The Windows version includes everything on Android plus desktop editing tools. On Android, I left out a photo editor because every phone already has one, even the Gallery app. On Windows, you get quick adjustments (like Dehaze and Clarity), complex repairs with individual frame adjustments, and 16-bit TIFF or linear DNG export so you don't finish tonal edits on an 8-bit JPG.

Next to the Windows download there is also a Lightroom Classic plug-in. Develop one frame, sync white balance, exposure, and tone across the set so seams match, and export via the zenpano plug-in. It writes 16-bit TIFFs with gimbal metadata, launches zenpano, and starts the stitch. When you close zenpano, the sphere returns to your Lightroom catalog as a JPG and, with a licence, as a 16-bit TIFF.
